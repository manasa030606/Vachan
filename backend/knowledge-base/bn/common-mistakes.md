# Common mistakes English speakers make in Bengali

## Using the wrong "you" — আপনি, তুমি, তুই

<!-- topic: politeness; level: beginner -->

The most common beginner mistake in Bengali is using the wrong word for "you". English has one "you"; Bengali has three: আপনি (aapni, polite) for elders, strangers, teachers, shopkeepers and anyone you have just met; তুমি (tumi, familiar) for friends, family and people your age or younger; তুই (tui, intimate) only for very close friends, younger siblings and small children. Calling a shopkeeper or an older person তুমি sounds rude, and using তুই with someone you don't know well is insulting. When unsure, always choose আপনি: আপনি কেমন আছেন? (aapni kemon aachhen?). People will tell you when to switch: আমাকে তুমি বলো (aamaake tumi bolo, "call me tumi").

## Forgetting to change the verb ending with "you"

<!-- topic: politeness; level: beginner -->

In Bengali, the verb ending must match the "you" you chose. A common mistake is mixing them, like আপনি কেমন আছো (wrong). Correct pairs: আপনি কেমন আছেন? (aapni kemon aachhen?), তুমি কেমন আছো? (tumi kemon aachho?), তুই কেমন আছিস? (tui kemon aachhis?). With খাওয়া (to eat): আপনি খান (aapni khaan), তুমি খাও (tumi khaao), তুই খাস (tui khaash). Commands too: বসুন (boshun) / বসো (bosho) / বস (bosh). Because the ending already shows who you mean, Bengalis often drop the pronoun: কেমন আছেন? (kemon aachhen?) is clearly polite.

## Expecting gender agreement — Bengali verbs have none

<!-- topic: gender -->

Learners who know Hindi often try to change Bengali verbs for gender. Bengali has no grammatical gender: the verb is the same for men and women. সে গেল (she gelo) = "he went" or "she went"; আমি যাচ্ছি (aami jaachchhi, "I am going") is said by everyone. Adjectives don't change either: ভালো ছেলে (bhaalo chhele, good boy), ভালো মেয়ে (bhaalo meye, good girl). Even the pronouns সে, ও and উনি mean both "he" and "she". What does change is a few nouns: ছাত্র / ছাত্রী (chhaatro / chhaatri, male / female student), শিক্ষক / শিক্ষিকা (shikkhok / shikkhikaa, male / female teacher).

## Putting the verb in the middle — word order

<!-- topic: word-order; level: beginner -->

English speakers often keep English order (subject – verb – object) in Bengali. Bengali puts the verb at the end: আমি ভাত খাই (aami bhaat khaai, "I eat rice"), not আমি খাই ভাত. Time and place usually come early, after the subject: আমি রোজ কলেজে যাই (aami roj koleje jaai, "I go to college every day"). Bengali uses endings and postpositions after nouns where English uses prepositions before them: বাড়িতে (baarite, at home), বন্ধুর সঙ্গে (bondhur shonge, with a friend), টেবিলের ওপর (tebiler opor, on the table). Question words also stay in place: আপনি কোথায় থাকেন? (aapni kothaay thaaken?).

## Adding a word for "is" — when Bengali drops it

<!-- topic: to-be -->

English speakers often look for a word for "is" in every Bengali sentence. In the present tense, "X is Y" needs no verb: আমি ছাত্র (aami chhaatro, "I am a student"), এটা আমার বই (etaa aamaar boi, "this is my book"). Adding হয় (hoy) here, as in আমি ছাত্র হই, sounds wrong. But use আছে (aachhe) for location, existence and states: আমি বাড়িতে আছি (aami baarite aachhi, "I am at home"), আমি ভালো আছি (aami bhaalo aachhi, "I am fine"). And use নয় (noy) for "is not": এটা আমার বই নয় (etaa aamaar boi noy). In the past the verb returns: ছিল (chhilo, "was").

## Saying "I am hungry" word for word

<!-- topic: feelings -->

A literal translation of "I am hungry" gives আমি ক্ষুধার্ত (aami khudhaarto), which is grammatically right but sounds like a book. Bengali expresses hunger, thirst, sleepiness and many feelings with "to me" (আমার, aamaar) and a verb: আমার খিদে পেয়েছে (aamaar khide peyechhe, "I'm hungry"), আমার তেষ্টা পেয়েছে (aamaar teshtaa peyechhe, "I'm thirsty"), আমার ঘুম পাচ্ছে (aamaar ghum paachchhe, "I'm sleepy"), আমার ঠান্ডা লাগছে (aamaar thaandaa laagchhe, "I feel cold"). Likes work the same way: আমার এটা ভালো লাগে (aamaar etaa bhaalo laage, "I like this"), not আমি এটা পছন্দ (wrong).

## Translating "I have" with a verb

<!-- topic: possession -->

Bengali has no verb "to have", so learners invent one. The correct pattern is আমার (aamaar, "of me") + thing + আছে (aachhe, "is there"): আমার একটা ভাই আছে (aamaar ektaa bhaai aachhe, "I have a brother"), আমার গাড়ি আছে (aamaar gaari aachhe, "I have a car"). Saying আমি একটা ভাই আছি is wrong (it means "I am a brother"!). The negative uses নেই (nei): আমার সময় নেই (aamaar shomoy nei, "I don't have time"), not আমার সময় আছে না. For an illness: আমার জ্বর হয়েছে (aamaar jor hoyechhe, "I have a fever").

## Reading the inherent vowel as "a" — অ is "o"

<!-- topic: pronunciation; skill: pronunciation; level: beginner -->

Learners who know Hindi or Sanskrit often read the Bengali inherent vowel as "a". In Bengali it is "o" (like the o in "hot"): ক is "ko", মন is "mon" (mind), কলম is "kolom" (pen), নমস্কার is "nomoshkaar". At the end of many words it is silent: নাম (naam, name), দুধ (dudh, milk). In the Vachan alphabet lessons this vowel অ is shown as "ô"; in words it is written "o". Also, শ, ষ and স are mostly "sh": সাত (shaat, seven), and ব is always "b", never "v": বাবা (baabaa, father).

## Long and short vowels — spelling, not sound

<!-- topic: vowel-length; skill: pronunciation -->

English speakers sometimes try hard to make ই/ঈ and উ/ঊ sound different in Bengali. In everyday Bengali each pair sounds almost the same, so don't worry about the length when speaking: দিন (din, day) and নীল (nil, blue) have the same "i". But spelling matters in writing: কি (ki) is the yes/no question marker, while কী (ki) means "what". The real long-vs-short difference that does matter is অ (o) versus আ (aa): কাল (kaal, yesterday / tomorrow) versus কল (kol, tap / machine), মামা (maamaa, uncle) versus মমতা (momotaa, affection).

## Dental and retroflex t and d

<!-- topic: retroflex; skill: pronunciation -->

English has one t and one d, made between the two Bengali sets. Bengali dental ত, দ (tongue against the back of the upper teeth) and retroflex ট, ড (tongue tip curled back) are different sounds, and they can change meaning: তাক (taak, shelf) versus টাক (taak, bald patch). Dental examples: তুমি (tumi, you), দুধ (dudh, milk), দশ (dosh, ten). Retroflex examples: টাকা (taakaa, money), ডাল (daal, lentils), ঠিক (thik, right). Vachan's romanization writes both as "t" and "d", so check the Bengali letter. Tip: English t is closer to the retroflex ট, so practise the dental ত carefully.

## পরা vs পড়া — wear vs read

<!-- topic: spelling; skill: script -->

Two Bengali verbs sound the same, "poraa", but are spelled differently: পরা (poraa, to wear) with র, and পড়া (poraa, to read / study / fall) with ড়. Examples: আমি শাড়ি পরি (aami shaari pori, "I wear a saree"), আমি বই পড়ি (aami boi pori, "I read books"). Mixing them up is a very common spelling mistake, even for native speakers. The same pair appears in other words: বাড়ি (baari, house) has ড়, but বারি (baari, water, literary) has র. When you learn a word with an "r" sound, also learn whether it is র or ড়.

## কাল means both yesterday and tomorrow

<!-- topic: time -->

Beginners are often confused because Bengali কাল (kaal) means both "yesterday" and "tomorrow". The verb tense decides: কাল আসব (kaal aashbo, "I'll come tomorrow"), কাল এসেছিলাম (kaal eshechhilaam, "I came yesterday"). A common mistake is pairing কাল with the wrong tense and confusing the listener. If you want to be extra clear, use আগামীকাল (aagaamikaal, tomorrow) or গতকাল (gotokaal, yesterday). The same applies to পরশু (porshu, day after tomorrow / day before yesterday). Example: কাল ছুটি ছিল, কাল আবার অফিস (kaal chhuti chhilo, kaal aabaar ophis) = "Yesterday was a holiday; tomorrow it's office again."

## Saying "drink" with পান করা

<!-- topic: food -->

English speakers look up "drink" and find পান করা (paan koraa), then say আমি জল পান করি. That sounds like a textbook. In everyday Bengali people "eat" their drinks with খাওয়া (khaaoyaa): জল খাও (jol khaao, "drink water"), চা খাবেন? (chaa khaaben?, "will you have tea?"), আমি কফি খাই না (aami kophi khaai naa, "I don't drink coffee"). Medicine is also "eaten": ওষুধ খাওয়া (oshudh khaaoyaa, to take medicine). Keep পান করা for formal writing and signs.

## Over-using ধন্যবাদ and দয়া করে

<!-- topic: politeness; skill: culture -->

English speakers say "please" and "thank you" constantly; in Bengali, ধন্যবাদ (dhonnobaad, thank you) and দয়া করে (doyaa kore, please) sound formal if used all the time. Politeness in Bengali comes mostly from the আপনি verb form and small words like একটু (ektu, "a little"): একটু জল দেবেন? (ektu jol deben?, "could I have some water?") is polite without দয়া করে. Between family and close friends, saying ধন্যবাদ can even feel distant; a smile or English "thanks" is more natural. Do thank shopkeepers, hosts and strangers who help you: অনেক ধন্যবাদ (onek dhonnobaad).

## দাদা is not grandfather

<!-- topic: family; skill: culture; level: beginner -->

Learners who know Hindi often think দাদা (daadaa) means grandfather. In Bengali, দাদা means "elder brother" and is also the polite way to address any slightly older man, such as a shopkeeper or driver: দাদা, এটা কত? (daadaa, etaa koto?, "how much is this?"). Grandfather is দাদু (daadu); father's father is also ঠাকুরদা (thaakurdaa). (In Muslim families, however, দাদা can mean the paternal grandfather.) Similarly, দিদি (didi) is elder sister, and দিদিমা (didimaa) is the maternal grandmother.

## Saying যাচ্ছি when leaving — use আসি

<!-- topic: greetings; skill: culture -->

When leaving someone's home, English speakers translate "I'm going" as আমি যাচ্ছি (aami jaachchhi). Grammatically fine, but Bengalis avoid it when saying goodbye because it sounds final. Instead they say আসি (aashi, literally "I come") or আসছি (aashchhi), implying they will come back. The host replies আবার আসবেন (aabaar aashben, "please come again") or সাবধানে যাবেন (shaabdhaane jaaben, "go carefully"). At night, before leaving, people still say আসি; শুভ রাত্রি (shubho raatri, good night) is formal and English "good night" is more common.

## Two words for "no" — না, নেই and নয় (naa, nei, noy)

<!-- topic: negation -->

Bengali has three "no / not" words that learners mix up. না (naa) is "no" as an answer and "not" after a verb: না, খাব না (naa, khaabo naa, "no, I won't eat"). নেই (nei) means "there isn't / don't have / isn't here": জল নেই (jol nei, "there's no water"), উনি বাড়িতে নেই (uni baarite nei, "he/she isn't at home"). নয় (noy) means "is not (something)": এটা আমার বই নয় (etaa aamaar boi noy, "this isn't my book"). Common mistakes: জল না for "no water" (should be জল নেই), and আমি বাড়িতে না for "I'm not at home" (better আমি বাড়িতে নেই). In casual speech না often replaces নয়, but never নেই.

## কি vs কী — question marker vs "what"

<!-- topic: questions; skill: script -->

Bengali writes two words that both sound "ki". কী (ki, with long ী) means "what": এটা কী? (etaa ki?, "what is this?"), তুমি কী খাবে? (tumi ki khaabe?, "what will you eat?"). কি (ki, with short ি) is the yes/no question marker: তুমি কি খাবে? (tumi ki khaabe?, "will you eat?"). The two sentences differ only in spelling, and in speech by stress: "what" কী is stressed, the marker কি is light. Answers differ too: to কী you give information (ভাত, bhaat, "rice"); to কি you answer হ্যাঁ / না. Many writers mix them up, so read the context.

## কখন vs কবে — two kinds of "when"

<!-- topic: questions -->

Learners often use one word for every "when" in Bengali. কখন (kokhon) asks the time of day: সিনেমা কখন শুরু? (sinemaa kokhon shuru?, "when does the film start?"), কখন আসবে? (kokhon aashbe?, "what time will you come?"). কবে (kobe) asks which day or date: তুমি কবে আসবে? (tumi kobe aashbe?, "which day will you come?"), পরীক্ষা কবে? (porikkhaa kobe?, "when is the exam?"). Answers match: কখন → বিকেল চারটে (bikel chaarte, 4 pm); কবে → সোমবার (shombaar, Monday). For an exact clock time also use কটায় (kotaay, "at what time").

## জানা vs চেনা — two kinds of "know"

<!-- topic: verbs -->

Bengali has two verbs for "to know". জানা (jaanaa) is knowing a fact or skill: আমি জানি (aami jaani, "I know"), আমি সাঁতার জানি (aami shaantaar jaani, "I know how to swim"), রাস্তাটা জানেন? (raastaataa jaanen?, "do you know the way?"). চেনা (chenaa) is knowing or recognising a person or place: আমি ওকে চিনি (aami oke chini, "I know him/her"), আপনি আমাকে চেনেন? (aapni aamaake chenen?, "do you know me?"), আমি এই জায়গাটা চিনি না (aami ei jaaygaataa chini naa, "I don't know this place"). Saying আমি ওকে জানি for "I know him" is a typical learner mistake.

## ভালো লাগা vs ভালোবাসা — like vs love

<!-- topic: feelings -->

Learners sometimes use ভালোবাসি (bhaalobaashi, "I love") for everything they like. In Bengali, ভালো লাগা (bhaalo laagaa) is "to like / enjoy": আমার চা ভালো লাগে (aamaar chaa bhaalo laage, "I like tea"). ভালোবাসা (bhaalobaashaa) is "to love", deeper and often romantic: আমি তোমাকে ভালোবাসি (aami tomaake bhaalobaashi, "I love you"). Saying ভালোবাসি about food or hobbies is possible (আমি গান গাইতে ভালোবাসি, aami gaan gaaite bhaalobaashi, "I love singing"), but saying it to a person is a declaration of love. Note the grammar: ভালো লাগে uses আমার (to me); ভালোবাসি uses আমি (I).
