# Beginner Bengali grammar

## Word order: subject – object – verb

<!-- level: beginner -->

Bengali sentences put the verb at the end. The usual order is subject – object – verb (SOV), unlike English subject – verb – object. আমি ভাত খাই (aami bhaat khaai) is literally "I rice eat" = "I eat rice". The negative না comes after the verb: আমি ভাত খাই না (aami bhaat khaai naa, "I don't eat rice"). Simple "X is Y" sentences in the present need no verb at all: আমি ছাত্র (aami chhaatro, "I am a student"), আমার নাম আশা (aamaar naam Asha, "my name is Asha").

## Personal pronouns

<!-- topic: pronouns; level: beginner -->

Bengali pronouns: আমি (aami, I), তুই (tui, you — very intimate), তুমি (tumi, you — familiar), আপনি (aapni, you — polite), সে (she, he or she), তিনি (tini, he or she — respectful), ও (o, he or she, nearby), উনি (uni, he or she — respectful, nearby), আমরা (aamraa, we), তোমরা (tomraa, you — plural, familiar), আপনারা (aapnaaraa, you — plural, polite), তারা (taaraa, they). Note that Bengali সে is pronounced "she" but means "he" or "she": Bengali pronouns do not show gender.

## Three levels of "you": তুই, তুমি, আপনি

<!-- topic: politeness; level: beginner -->

Bengali has three words for "you", and the verb ending changes with each. তুই (tui) is very intimate, used with very close friends, small children or younger siblings; তুমি (tumi) is familiar, used with friends, family and people your age; আপনি (aapni) is polite, used with elders, strangers, teachers and shopkeepers. With খাওয়া (to eat): তুই খাস (tui khaash), তুমি খাও (tumi khaao), আপনি খান (aapni khaan) — all mean "you eat". With আছ (to be): তুমি কেমন আছো? but আপনি কেমন আছেন?

## Possessives: my, your, our

<!-- topic: possessives; level: beginner -->

Bengali possessive words come before the noun: আমার (aamaar, my), তোর (tor, your — intimate), তোমার (tomaar, your — familiar), আপনার (aapnaar, your — polite), তার (taar, his / her), তাঁর (taar, his / her — respectful), আমাদের (aamaader, our), তোমাদের (tomaader, your — plural), তাদের (taader, their). Examples: আমার নাম (aamaar naam, my name), আপনার বাড়ি (aapnaar baari, your house), আমাদের দেশ (aamaader desh, our country). Unlike some Indian languages, Bengali has just one word for "our".

## Case endings (postpositions)

<!-- topic: postpositions -->

Bengali adds endings to nouns where English uses prepositions. -এ / -তে (-e / -te) means "in / at": ঘরে (ghore, in the room), বাড়িতে (baarite, at home). -কে (-ke) marks a person as the object or receiver: মাকে বলো (maake bolo, tell mother). -র / -এর (-r / -er) means "of": বাবার (baabaar, father's), রামের (Raamer, Ram's). থেকে (theke) means "from": কলকাতা থেকে (Kolkata theke, from Kolkata). সাথে or সঙ্গে (shaathe, shonge) means "with", after a noun in the -র form: মায়ের সাথে (maayer shaathe, with mother).

## Classifiers: -টা, -টি, -গুলো

<!-- topic: classifiers -->

Bengali often adds a classifier to nouns and numbers. -টা (-taa) and -টি (-ti) mean "the (one)": বইটা (boitaa, the book); -টি is a little more formal or affectionate: বইটি (boiti). With a number they mean "pieces of": একটা বই (ektaa boi, one book / a book), দুটো কলম (duto kolom, two pens). -গুলো (-gulo) means "the (many)": বইগুলো (boigulo, the books). These classifiers are a key part of natural Bengali.

## Plurals

<!-- topic: plurals -->

Bengali makes plurals in a few ways. For people it adds -রা / -এরা (-raa / -eraa): ছাত্ররা (chhaatroraa, the students), মেয়েরা (meyeraa, the girls). For things and animals it usually adds -গুলো (-gulo): বইগুলো (boigulo, the books). After a number or a word like অনেক (onek, many), Bengali does not add a plural ending: পাঁচটা বই (paanchtaa boi, five books), অনেক মানুষ (onek maanush, many people).

## "I want" and "I like" — আমার … চাই

<!-- topic: wanting -->

To say "I want" in Bengali, put the person in the possessive form and end with চাই (chaai): আমার জল চাই (aamaar jol chaai, "I want water"), literally "of me water is wanted". আপনার কী চাই? (aapnaar ki chaai?, "what do you want?"). Liking works the same way: আমার বাংলা ভালো লাগে (aamaar baanglaa bhaalo laage, "I like Bengali", literally "to me Bengali feels good"). Knowing uses a normal subject: আমি বাংলা জানি (aami baanglaa jaani, "I know Bengali").

## Asking questions

<!-- topic: questions -->

Bengali yes/no questions add কি (ki), usually after the subject: তুমি কি আসবে? (tumi ki aashbe?, "will you come?"). Question words: কী (ki, what), কে (ke, who), কোথায় (kothaay, where), কখন (kokhon, when), কেন (keno, why), কেমন (kemon, how), কত (koto, how much), কোনটা (kontaa, which). They usually go just before the verb: আপনি কোথায় যাচ্ছেন? (aapni kothaay jaachchhen?, "where are you going?"). In writing, কি is for yes/no questions and কী means "what".

## Saying "not" — না, নেই and নয়

<!-- topic: negation -->

Bengali puts না (naa) after the verb to make it negative: আমি জানি না (aami jaani naa, "I don't know"), আমি যাব না (aami jaabo naa, "I won't go"). নেই (nei) means "there isn't / doesn't have": জল নেই (jol nei, "there is no water"), আমার টাকা নেই (aamaar taakaa nei, "I don't have money"). নয় (noy) denies what something is: এটা আমার বাড়ি নয় (etaa aamaar baari noy, "this is not my house"). The past is negated with -নি: আমি যাইনি (aami jaaini, "I did not go").

## No gender in Bengali verbs

<!-- topic: gender; level: intermediate -->

Bengali has no grammatical gender. Verbs do not change for "he" or "she": সে গেল (she gelo) means "he went" or "she went". Adjectives do not change either: ভালো ছেলে (bhaalo chhele, good boy), ভালো মেয়ে (bhaalo meye, good girl). Instead, Bengali verbs change for person and for politeness: সে খায় (she khaay, he/she eats) but তিনি খান (tini khaan, he/she — respectful — eats). Some nouns still have separate female forms, such as ছাত্র (chhaatro, male student) and ছাত্রী (chhaatri, female student).
