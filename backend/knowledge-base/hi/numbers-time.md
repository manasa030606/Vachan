# Numbers, time and dates in Hindi

## Counting from 1 to 10 in Hindi

To count from one to ten in Hindi: 1 एक (ek), 2 दो (do), 3 तीन (teen), 4 चार (chaar), 5 पाँच (paanch), 6 छह (chhah, pronounced "chhe"; also written छः), 7 सात (saat), 8 आठ (aath), 9 नौ (nau), 10 दस (das). Zero is शून्य (shoonya), though people usually say ज़ीरो (zeero). Hindi has its own digits too — ० १ २ ३ ४ ५ ६ ७ ८ ९ — seen on some signs and in books, but most people write ordinary 0–9. Numbers go before the noun: एक चाय (ek chaay, one tea), दो टिकट (do tikat, two tickets), तीन दोस्त (teen dost, three friends).

## Numbers 11 to 20

Hindi numbers from 11 to 20: 11 ग्यारह (gyaarah), 12 बारह (baarah), 13 तेरह (terah), 14 चौदह (chaudah), 15 पंद्रह (pandrah), 16 सोलह (solah), 17 सत्रह (satrah), 18 अठारह (athaarah), 19 उन्नीस (unnees), 20 बीस (bees). Most of 11–18 end in -रह (-rah). 19 means "one less than twenty", and the same pattern runs through 29, 39 and so on: उनतीस (untees, 29), उनतालीस (untaalees, 39). Learn these well: they are used for clock times, ages and prices.

## Tens: 20, 30 … 100 — बीस, तीस … सौ

The tens in Hindi: 10 दस (das), 20 बीस (bees), 30 तीस (tees), 40 चालीस (chaalees), 50 पचास (pachaas), 60 साठ (saath), 70 सत्तर (sattar), 80 अस्सी (assee), 90 नब्बे (nabbe), 100 सौ (sau). Unlike English, every number from 11 to 99 is its own word and is not built simply as "twenty-one": 21 is इक्कीस (ikkees), 25 is पच्चीस (pachchees), 35 is पैंतीस (paintees). Because of this, many people in cities use English for numbers above twenty, especially for prices and phone numbers. Learning the tens and a few common numbers (25, 50, 75 पचहत्तर, pachhattar) is enough to start.

## Hundred, thousand, lakh, crore

Big numbers in Hindi: सौ (sau, hundred), एक सौ (ek sau, one hundred), दो सौ (do sau, 200), हज़ार (hazaar, thousand), दस हज़ार (das hazaar, 10,000), लाख (laakh, one hundred thousand = 1,00,000), करोड़ (karod, ten million = 1,00,00,000). Indians count large amounts in lakhs and crores, also in English, and place commas in that pattern. Examples: पाँच सौ रुपये (paanch sau rupaye, 500 rupees), दो हज़ार (do hazaar, 2,000), दस लाख (das laakh, one million). Fractions have special words: आधा (aadhaa, half), डेढ़ (dedh, 1½), ढाई (dhaaee, 2½), and साढ़े (saadhe) + number for "and a half" from 3½ on: साढ़े तीन (3½).

## First, second, third — ordinal numbers

Hindi ordinal numbers: पहला (pahlaa, first), दूसरा (doosraa, second), तीसरा (teesraa, third), चौथा (chauthaa, fourth), then add -वाँ (-vaan): पाँचवाँ (paanchvaan, fifth), सातवाँ (saatvaan, seventh), दसवाँ (dasvaan, tenth). Sixth is irregular: छठा (chhathaa). They agree like adjectives: पहला दिन (pahlaa din, first day), पहली बार (pahlee baar, first time — feminine), दूसरे साल में (doosre saal mein, in the second year). Floors: पहली मंज़िल (pahlee manzil, first floor), but in buildings people often say "फ़र्स्ट फ़्लोर".

## What time is it? — कितने बजे हैं? (kitne baje hain?)

<!-- topic: time; type: phrase -->

To ask the time in Hindi, say कितने बजे हैं? (kitne baje hain?), literally "how many (hours) have struck?". Very common in speech: टाइम क्या हुआ है? (taaim kyaa huaa hai?) or क्या टाइम हुआ है? (kyaa taaim huaa hai?, what's the time?). Politely to a stranger: सुनिए, टाइम क्या हुआ है? (suniye, taaim kyaa huaa hai?). The answer uses बजे: पाँच बजे हैं (paanch baje hain, it's five o'clock); for one o'clock the singular एक बजा है (ek bajaa hai). These questions are the same for men and women.

## Telling the time — बजे, सवा, साढ़े, पौने

<!-- topic: time -->

Hindi tells the time with बजे (baje, o'clock): तीन बजे (teen baje, three o'clock / at three). Quarters and halves have special words: सवा (savaa, quarter past): सवा चार (savaa chaar, 4:15); साढ़े (saadhe, half past): साढ़े चार (saadhe chaar, 4:30); पौने (paune, quarter to): पौने पाँच (paune paanch, 4:45). 1:30 is डेढ़ (dedh) and 2:30 is ढाई (dhaaee), not साढ़े. Exact minutes: चार बजकर दस मिनट (chaar bajkar das minat, 4:10); पाँच बजने में दस मिनट (paanch bajne mein das minat, ten to five). Many people simply read the digital time in English.

## Morning, afternoon, evening, night — am and pm

<!-- topic: time -->

Hindi has no "am / pm"; instead you add the part of the day: सुबह (subah, morning), दोपहर (dopahar, afternoon), शाम (shaam, evening), रात (raat, night). With a clock time use के: सुबह के आठ बजे (subah ke aath baje, 8 am), दोपहर के दो बजे (dopahar ke do baje, 2 pm), शाम के छह बजे (shaam ke chhah baje, 6 pm), रात के दस बजे (raat ke das baje, 10 pm). Without a time, use को: शाम को (shaam ko, in the evening), रात को (raat ko, at night); सुबह alone means "in the morning".

## At what time? — कितने बजे? (kitne baje?)

<!-- topic: time; type: phrase -->

To ask "at what time?" in Hindi, say कितने बजे? (kitne baje?). Examples: ट्रेन कितने बजे है? (tren kitne baje hai?, what time is the train?), तुम कितने बजे उठते हो? (tum kitne baje uthte ho?, what time do you get up?), मीटिंग कितने बजे है? (meeting kitne baje hai?). Answer with the time + बजे and no extra word for "at": मैं छह बजे उठता हूँ (main chhah baje uthtaa hoon, I get up at six) — a woman says उठती हूँ. "From … to …" is … से … तक (… se … tak): नौ से पाँच बजे तक (nau se paanch baje tak, from nine to five).

## Today, tomorrow, yesterday — आज, कल, परसों

<!-- topic: time -->

In Hindi आज (aaj) is today. कल (kal) means both "tomorrow" and "yesterday" — the verb tense tells you which: मैं कल आऊँगा (main kal aaoongaa, I will come tomorrow) vs मैं कल आया था (main kal aayaa thaa, I came yesterday); a woman says आऊँगी / आई थी. In the same way परसों (parson) means both "the day after tomorrow" and "the day before yesterday". Other words: अभी (abhee, right now), बाद में (baad mein, later), आजकल (aajkal, these days). If you must be explicit, say आने वाला कल (aane vaalaa kal, the coming kal) or बीता हुआ कल (beetaa huaa kal), but this is rare in conversation.

## Days of the week — सोमवार, मंगलवार …

<!-- topic: days -->

Hindi weekdays all end in -वार (-vaar, day): सोमवार (somvaar, Monday), मंगलवार (mangalvaar, Tuesday), बुधवार (budhvaar, Wednesday), गुरुवार (guruvaar, Thursday), शुक्रवार (shukravaar, Friday), शनिवार (shanivaar, Saturday), रविवार (ravivaar, Sunday). Everyday alternatives: इतवार (itvaar) for Sunday and वीरवार (veervaar) for Thursday. "On Monday" is सोमवार को (somvaar ko). "What day is it today?": आज कौन-सा दिन है? (aaj kaun-saa din hai?) — आज सोमवार है (aaj somvaar hai, today is Monday). Many people also just say the English names.

## Months and dates — महीना, तारीख़

<!-- topic: dates -->

In daily life Hindi uses the English month names, written in Devanagari: जनवरी (janvaree), फ़रवरी (farvaree), मार्च (maarch), अप्रैल (aprail), मई (maee), जून (joon), जुलाई (julaaee), अगस्त (agast), सितंबर (sitambar), अक्टूबर (aktoobar), नवंबर (navambar), दिसंबर (disambar). Traditional Hindu months like सावन (saavan) and कार्तिक (kaartik) are used for festivals. Dates use तारीख़ (taareekh): आज कौन-सी तारीख़ है? (aaj kaun-see taareekh hai?, what's the date today?) — आज पंद्रह तारीख़ है (aaj pandrah taareekh hai, today is the 15th). A date is said number + month: पंद्रह अगस्त (pandrah agast, 15 August).

## Week, month, year — this, next, last

<!-- topic: time -->

Hindi time-period words: दिन (din, day), हफ़्ता (haftaa, week; formal सप्ताह, saptaah), महीना (maheenaa, month), साल (saal, year; formal वर्ष, varsh). "This / next / last" are इस (is), अगले (agle), पिछले (pichhle): इस हफ़्ते (is hafte, this week), अगले महीने (agle maheene, next month), पिछले साल (pichhle saal, last year). "Every day" is हर दिन (har din) or रोज़ (roz). "For the past two days" is दो दिन से (do din se), and "for two days (ahead)" is दो दिन के लिए (do din ke lie). "How many days?": कितने दिन? (kitne din?).

## How old are you? — आपकी उम्र क्या है? (aapkee umr kyaa hai?)

<!-- type: phrase -->

To ask someone's age in Hindi: आपकी उम्र क्या है? (aapkee umr kyaa hai?, what is your age?) — उम्र (umr, pronounced "umar") is feminine, so आपकी. Also: आप कितने साल के हैं? (aap kitne saal ke hain?); to a woman कितने साल की हैं? (kitne saal kee hain?). Answer: a man says मैं बीस साल का हूँ (main bees saal kaa hoon, I am twenty) and a woman says मैं बीस साल की हूँ (main bees saal kee hoon). Gender-neutral: मेरी उम्र बीस साल है (meree umr bees saal hai). Asking adults their age is not considered rude in India.

## Phone numbers — फ़ोन नंबर (fon nambar)

<!-- type: phrase -->

To ask for a phone number in Hindi: आपका फ़ोन नंबर क्या है? (aapkaa fon nambar kyaa hai?, what is your phone number?); casually तुम्हारा नंबर क्या है? (tumhaaraa nambar kyaa hai?). Also common: अपना नंबर दीजिए (apnaa nambar deejiye, please give me your number). Phone numbers are usually read digit by digit, very often in English: "nine eight one…". In Hindi digits: नौ आठ एक … (nau aath ek …). Repeated digits may be said as डबल (dabal): डबल पाँच (double five). Call me: मुझे फ़ोन कीजिए (mujhe fon keejiye).
