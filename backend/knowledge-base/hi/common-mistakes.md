# Common mistakes English speakers make in Hindi

## Using तुम or तू with strangers

<!-- topic: pronouns; skill: conversation -->

The most noticeable beginner mistake in Hindi is using the wrong "you". आप (aap) is the polite form for strangers, elders, teachers, shopkeepers and anyone you have just met; तुम (tum) is for friends, younger people and family; तू (too) is only for very close friends, small children or prayer, and sounds rude to a stranger. Each one changes the verb: आप कैसे हैं? (aap kaise hain?), तुम कैसे हो? (tum kaise ho?), तू कैसा है? (too kaisaa hai?). Commands too: बैठिए (baithiye, polite), बैठो (baitho, casual), बैठ (baith, intimate). Rule of thumb: start with आप and switch only when the other person invites you to.

## Forgetting your own gender in verbs

<!-- topic: gender -->

In Hindi many verbs show the speaker's gender, so men and women say "I" sentences differently — a common surprise for English speakers. A man says मैं जाता हूँ (main jaataa hoon, I go), मैं थक गया हूँ (main thak gayaa hoon, I'm tired), मैं आऊँगा (main aaoongaa, I'll come). A woman says मैं जाती हूँ (main jaatee hoon), मैं थक गई हूँ (main thak gaee hoon), मैं आऊँगी (main aaoongee). The pattern: -आ (-aa) endings are masculine, -ई (-ee) endings are feminine. Words like ठीक (theek, fine) and ख़ुश (khush, happy) don't change, and sentences starting with मुझे (mujhe) are the same for everyone.

## "My" agrees with the thing, not with you

<!-- topic: possessives -->

English speakers often think मेरा / मेरी (meraa / meree, my) depends on the speaker's gender. In Hindi it agrees with the thing owned: मेरा भाई (meraa bhaaee, my brother — masculine), मेरी किताब (meree kitaab, my book — feminine), मेरे दोस्त (mere dost, my friends — plural). So a woman also says मेरा नाम आशा है (meraa naam Asha hai), because नाम is masculine, and a man says मेरी माँ (meree maa, my mother). The same rule applies to आपका / आपकी (aapkaa / aapkee, your) and का / की / के (kaa / kee / ke, 's).

## Putting the verb in the middle

<!-- topic: word-order -->

English word order is subject – verb – object, but Hindi is subject – object – verb, with the verb at the end. Beginners often say "मैं खाता हूँ खाना", which sounds wrong; the correct sentence is मैं खाना खाता हूँ (main khaanaa khaataa hoon, I eat food). Time and place usually come early: मैं कल दिल्ली जाऊँगा (main kal Dillee jaaoongaa, I will go to Delhi tomorrow). Question words stay in place instead of moving to the front: आप कहाँ जा रहे हैं? (aap kahaan jaa rahe hain?, where are you going?). Postpositions come after the noun: घर में (ghar mein, in the house), not "में घर".

## Dropping है (hai, is)

<!-- topic: to-be -->

Some languages can leave out "is", but in normal Hindi statements है (hai) / हूँ (hoon) / हैं (hain) must be there: मैं छात्र हूँ (main chhaatr hoon, I am a student), यह मेरा घर है (yah meraa ghar hai, this is my house). Saying just "मैं छात्र" sounds incomplete. The only common place where है disappears is after नहीं (nahin) in the present tense: मैं मांस नहीं खाता (main maans nahin khaataa, I don't eat meat), and in very quick casual questions like कहाँ जा रहे? (kahaan jaa rahe?, where (are you) going?). Also make है agree: plural or respectful subjects take हैं.

## Long and short vowels

<!-- topic: vowel-length; skill: pronunciation -->

English speakers often don't hold long Hindi vowels long enough, and vowel length changes meaning. कम (kam) is "less" but काम (kaam) is "work"; दिन (din) is "day" but दीन (deen) is "poor / humble"; मिल (mil, meet — stem) and मील (meel, mile). In Vachan's romanization doubled letters mark long vowels: aa, ee, oo. Also, the short अ (a) is a light "uh" sound, as in English "but", not the "a" of "cat". Practise pairs aloud and follow the romanization: नाम is "naam", not "nam".

## Retroflex and dental t / d

<!-- topic: retroflex; skill: pronunciation -->

English t and d sit between the two Hindi sets, so learners often mix them. Dental त थ द ध are made with the tongue touching the back of the upper teeth: तीन (teen, three), दाल (daal, lentils). Retroflex ट ठ ड ढ are made with the tongue tip curled back to the roof of the mouth: टमाटर (tamaatar, tomato), डाल (daal, branch / to put). So दाल (lentils) and डाल (branch) differ only in this sound. Using English t for both is understood in context, but practising the difference makes you sound much more natural.

## Aspirated vs plain consonants

<!-- topic: aspiration; skill: pronunciation -->

Hindi separates sounds with and without a puff of air, and English speakers often miss it. पल (pal, moment) vs फल (phal, fruit); काना (kaanaa, one-eyed) vs खाना (khaanaa, food); बात (baat, matter / talk) vs भात (bhaat, cooked rice). In romanization the h shows aspiration: kh, gh, chh, th, dh, ph, bh. Important: ph is an aspirated p, not English f — फल is "p-hal". The English-like f sound is written फ़ with a dot (नुक़्ता), as in फ़ोन (fon, phone).

## Forgetting ने (ne) in the past tense

<!-- topic: past-tense; level: intermediate -->

In the Hindi simple past, verbs that take an object (eat, see, drink, buy, say) use ने (ne) on the subject, and the verb then agrees with the object. So "I ate" is मैंने खाया (mainne khaayaa), not "मैं खाया". मैंने चाय पी (mainne chaay pee, I drank tea — पी is feminine because चाय is), मैंने किताब ख़रीदी (mainne kitaab khareedee, I bought a book). Verbs of motion like जाना (to go) and आना (to come) never take ने: मैं गया (main gayaa, I went), a woman मैं गई (main gaee). A useful tip: with ने, men and women say the same sentence.

## Literal translations that sound odd

<!-- skill: conversation -->

Some English phrases don't translate word for word into Hindi. "I am cold" is मुझे ठंड लग रही है (mujhe thand lag rahee hai, cold is striking me), not "मैं ठंडा हूँ", which sounds like "I am cold to the touch". "I am hungry" is best मुझे भूख लगी है (mujhe bhookh lagee hai). "I have a brother" is मेरा एक भाई है (meraa ek bhaaee hai) — there is no verb "have". "I like it" is मुझे पसंद है (mujhe pasand hai), not "मैं पसंद करता हूँ" for simple likes. "I know Hindi" is मुझे हिंदी आती है (mujhe hindee aatee hai, Hindi comes to me). "Good morning" and "good night" are usually said in English or replaced by नमस्ते.

## Overusing कृपया (please) and धन्यवाद (thank you)

<!-- topic: politeness; skill: culture -->

English speakers tend to put "please" and "thank you" everywhere. In Hindi, कृपया (kripyaa, please) is formal and mostly written; in speech the polite verb ending already does the job: पानी दीजिए (paani deejiye, water, please). Similarly धन्यवाद (dhanyavaad) or शुक्रिया (shukriyaa) is right with strangers, shopkeepers and helpers, but thanking close family for small things can feel distant or formal. Among family and friends warmth is shown through tone, अरे (are) and यार (yaar), or simply a smile.

## Respectful plural for elders

<!-- topic: politeness -->

When talking about a respected person in Hindi — a parent, teacher, elder or boss — use the plural even for one person: मेरे पिताजी डॉक्टर हैं (mere pitaajee doktar hain, my father is a doctor), not "मेरा पिताजी … है". Likewise ये मेरी माँ हैं (ye meree maa hain, this is my mother) and वे आ रहे हैं (ve aa rahe hain, he is coming — respectful). Using the singular है for an elder sounds disrespectful. For friends, children and younger siblings the singular is normal: वह मेरा दोस्त है (vah meraa dost hai, he is my friend).

## Confusing कल (yesterday and tomorrow)

<!-- topic: time -->

कल (kal) means both "yesterday" and "tomorrow" in Hindi, and learners often panic about it. The verb tense always makes it clear: कल मिलेंगे (kal milenge, see you tomorrow — future) vs मैं कल आया था (main kal aayaa thaa, I came yesterday — past). Just get the tense right and कल takes care of itself. The same goes for परसों (parson, the day after tomorrow / the day before yesterday). Remember to match gender in the past: a woman says मैं कल आई थी (main kal aaee thee).

## नहीं, मत and ना — three words for "no / not"

<!-- topic: negation; skill: grammar -->

Hindi has several words that translate as "no" or "not", and learners mix them up. नहीं (nahin) is the normal "no" and "not": नहीं, धन्यवाद (nahin, dhanyavaad, no, thank you), मैं नहीं जानता (main nahin jaantaa, I don't know). मत (mat) is only for commands — "don't!": मत जाओ (mat jaao, don't go), चिंता मत कीजिए (chintaa mat keejiye, don't worry). ना (naa) is a casual spoken "no" (ना, मुझे नहीं चाहिए, naa, mujhe nahin chaahiye, nah, I don't want it) and, at the end of a sentence, a tag meaning "right? / please": आओ ना (aao naa, do come). न (na) is a polite or written negative: न जाइए (na jaaiye, please don't go), कृपया धूम्रपान न करें (please do not smoke).

## धन्यवाद vs शुक्रिया — two words for thank you

<!-- topic: politeness; skill: vocabulary -->

Hindi has two common words for "thank you", and both are correct everywhere. धन्यवाद (dhanyavaad) comes from Sanskrit and sounds a little more formal — it is the word on signs and in speeches. शुक्रिया (shukriyaa) comes from Urdu / Persian and sounds a little warmer and more conversational. "Thanks a lot": बहुत-बहुत धन्यवाद (bahut-bahut dhanyavaad) or बहुत-बहुत शुक्रिया (bahut-bahut shukriyaa). English "thank you" and "thanks" are also extremely common in cities. The reply to any of them is कोई बात नहीं (koee baat nahin, you're welcome).

## यह vs वह — this and that (and he / she)

<!-- topic: pronouns; skill: grammar -->

यह (yah, spoken ये ye) means "this" and also "he / she / it" for someone or something near; वह (vah, spoken वो vo) means "that" and "he / she / it" for someone or something further away. Hindi does not separate "he" and "she" — the verb shows gender: वह आता है (vah aataa hai, he comes), वह आती है (vah aatee hai, she comes). Examples: यह मेरी किताब है (yah meree kitaab hai, this is my book), वह मेरा दोस्त है (vah meraa dost hai, that is / he is my friend). For respect, use the plural forms ये / वे (ye / ve) with हैं: ये मेरी माँ हैं (ye meree maa hain, this is my mother).

## खाना (food) vs खाना (to eat), and other double-meaning words

<!-- topic: vocabulary; skill: vocabulary -->

Some common Hindi words have two meanings, which confuses beginners. खाना (khaanaa) is both "food" and "to eat": खाना खाना (khaanaa khaanaa, to eat food). सोना (sonaa) is "to sleep" and "gold". पता (pataa) is "address" and "known": मुझे पता है (mujhe pataa hai, I know), आपका पता क्या है? (aapkaa pataa kyaa hai?, what is your address?). कल (kal) is both "yesterday" and "tomorrow". आम (aam) is "mango" and "common / ordinary". बस (bas) is "bus" and "enough / that's all": बस, धन्यवाद (bas, dhanyavaad, that's enough, thanks). Context always makes the meaning clear.

Also asked as: words with two meanings in Hindi, khana food or eat, what does bas mean, confusing Hindi words

## चाहिए vs चाहता हूँ — wanting a thing vs wanting to do

<!-- topic: wanting; skill: grammar -->

Two Hindi ways of "wanting" are often confused. मुझे … चाहिए (mujhe … chaahiye) is for wanting or needing a THING and never changes for gender: मुझे पानी चाहिए (mujhe paani chaahiye, I want water). मैं … चाहता हूँ (main … chaahtaa hoon) is for wanting to DO something, with the -ना verb, and changes for gender: मैं घर जाना चाहता हूँ (main ghar jaanaa chaahtaa hoon, I want to go home); a woman says चाहती हूँ (chaahtee hoon). "मैं पानी चाहिए" is wrong. A very common everyday alternative for wanting to do something is मुझे … है: मुझे घर जाना है (mujhe ghar jaanaa hai, I want / need to go home).
