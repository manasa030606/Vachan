# Malayalam culture and context

## Showing respect: ചേട്ടൻ, ചേച്ചി and സാർ

In Malayalam, people often address others with family words instead of names. ചേട്ടൻ (chettan, elder brother) and ചേച്ചി (chechi, elder sister) are used not only in the family but also for strangers a little older than you — a shopkeeper, a bus conductor or an auto driver. When calling someone, they become ചേട്ടാ (chettaa) and ചേച്ചീ (chechee). Teachers and officials are called സാർ (saar) or ടീച്ചർ (teacher). Older people may call a younger person മോനേ (mone, son) or മോളേ (mole, daughter) affectionately. The North Indian ending -ജി (-ji) is not typical in Kerala.

## Where Malayalam is spoken

<!-- topic: about-language -->

Malayalam is the official language of Kerala and is also spoken in Lakshadweep and in Mahé (part of Puducherry). There are large Malayali communities in other Indian cities and abroad, especially in the Gulf countries. Malayalam is a Dravidian language, closely related to Tamil, and it borrows many words from Sanskrit. The Government of India declared Malayalam a classical language in 2013. A speaker of Malayalam is called a മലയാളി (malayaali). Fun fact: "Malayalam" is a palindrome in English letters — it reads the same backwards.

## Festivals: Onam and Vishu

<!-- topic: festivals; level: elementary -->

ഓണം (Onam) is the biggest festival of Kerala and of Malayalam speakers everywhere, a harvest festival in August or September that welcomes the legendary king Mahabali. Families make a പൂക്കളം (pookalam), a carpet of flower petals in front of the house, and share the ഓണസദ്യ (onasadya), a large vegetarian feast served on a banana leaf. People greet each other with ഓണാശംസകൾ (onaashamsakal, Onam greetings). വിഷു (Vishu), in mid-April, begins with the വിഷുക്കണി (vishukkani): the first auspicious sight of the day, an arrangement of yellow konna flowers, fruits, rice, a lamp and gold. Elders give children money called കൈനീട്ടം (kaineettam).

## Everyday etiquette

<!-- topic: etiquette -->

Some useful Malayalam etiquette: greet elders with നമസ്കാരം (namaskaaram) and folded hands; take your shoes off before entering a home or temple; offer and receive things with the right hand. Traditional meals are eaten with the right hand, often from a banana leaf. When you are offered more food, a polite "enough" is മതി (mathi). Guests are usually offered ചായ (chaaya, tea) or കാപ്പി (kaappi, coffee) first, and it is polite to accept something small.
