# Common mistakes English speakers make in Malayalam

## Formal and casual "you" — നിങ്ങൾ vs നീ

<!-- topic: politeness; skill: conversation; level: beginner -->

Malayalam has a polite and a casual "you". നിങ്ങൾ (ningal) is polite (and also plural): use it with strangers, elders, teachers, shopkeepers and anyone you are unsure about. നീ (nee) is casual: only for close friends, younger siblings and children. താങ്കൾ (thaankal) is very formal, for speeches and letters. The forms follow through: നിങ്ങൾക്ക് / നിനക്ക് (ningalkku / ninakku, to you), നിങ്ങളുടെ / നിന്റെ (ningalude / ninte, your). Example: നിങ്ങൾക്ക് സുഖമാണോ? (polite) vs നിനക്ക് സുഖമാണോ? (casual), both "how are you?". The common mistake is using നീ with an elder, which sounds rude; with elders many people use a title like ചേട്ടൻ (chettan) instead.

## Formal and casual commands — വരൂ vs വാ

<!-- topic: politeness; skill: conversation; level: beginner -->

Malayalam commands also come in casual and polite forms, and mixing them up is a common mistake. Casual: വാ (vaa, come), ഇരിക്ക് (irikku, sit), താ (thaa, give me), പറ (para, tell) — fine with friends and children. Polite, ending in -ഊ (-oo): വരൂ (varoo), ഇരിക്കൂ (irikkoo), തരൂ (tharoo), പറയൂ (parayoo) — use with elders, strangers and in shops. Even softer is a question in -ആമോ? (-aamo?): തരാമോ? (tharaamo?, could you give?). Saying വാ or താ to a shopkeeper or an elder sounds rude.

## Two words for "no" — അല്ല (alla) vs ഇല്ല (illa)

<!-- topic: negation; level: beginner -->

Learners often confuse Malayalam's two "no" words. അല്ല (alla) means "is not" — it denies what something is: ഇത് ചായ അല്ല (ithu chaaya alla, this is not tea); ഞാൻ ഡോക്ടറല്ല (njaan doctoralla, I am not a doctor). ഇല്ല (illa) means "there isn't / didn't / won't": ചായ ഇല്ല (chaaya illa, there is no tea); ഞാൻ പോയില്ല (njaan poyilla, I didn't go). A third word, വേണ്ട (venda), means "I don't want": ചായ വേണ്ട (chaaya venda, no tea for me). Answer "Is this yours?" with അല്ല, but "Do you have…?" with ഇല്ല.

## Saying "I am hungry" with ഞാൻ — use എനിക്ക്

<!-- topic: dative -->

A very common beginner mistake in Malayalam is starting feelings and needs with ഞാൻ (njaan, I). Malayalam uses the "to me" form എനിക്ക് (enikku) for hunger, thirst, wanting, liking, knowing, illness and many feelings. Wrong: ഞാൻ വിശക്കുന്നു. Right: എനിക്ക് വിശക്കുന്നു (enikku vishakkunnu, I am hungry). Likewise എനിക്ക് വെള്ളം വേണം (enikku vellam venam, I want water), എനിക്ക് ഇഷ്ടമാണ് (enikku ishtamaanu, I like it), എനിക്ക് അറിയില്ല (enikku ariyilla, I don't know), എനിക്ക് പനിയുണ്ട് (enikku paniyundu, I have a fever). Actions still use ഞാൻ: ഞാൻ പോകുന്നു (njaan pokunnu, I go).

## Two words for "give" — തരുക vs കൊടുക്കുക

<!-- topic: verbs -->

Malayalam has two verbs for "give", and English speakers often mix them up. തരുക (tharuka) is giving towards the speaker (me / us): ഒരു ചായ തരൂ (oru chaaya tharoo, give me a tea). കൊടുക്കുക (kodukkuka) is giving to someone else: ഇത് അമ്മയ്ക്ക് കൊടുക്കൂ (ithu ammaykku kodukkoo, give this to mother). So "give me" is never കൊടുക്കൂ. തരുക also appears inside other verbs meaning "for me": കാണിച്ചുതരൂ (kaanichchutharoo, show me), പറഞ്ഞുതരൂ (paranjutharoo, tell me / explain to me).

## Commonly confused word pairs

<!-- topic: vocabulary; skill: vocabulary -->

Malayalam word pairs that beginners mix up: വെള്ളം (vellam, water) vs വെള്ള (vella, white); ആര് (aaru, who) vs ആറ് (aaru, six), different r letters; പുലി (puli, leopard) vs പുളി (puli, sour / tamarind), ല vs ള; കറി (kari, curry) vs കരി (kari, charcoal), റ vs ര; മല (mala, mountain) vs മാല (maala, necklace), short vs long a; ഉപ്പ് (uppu, salt) vs ഉപ്പ (uppa, father in some Muslim families); മതി (mathi, enough) vs മത്തി (mathi, sardine). The romanization often looks the same, so check the Malayalam script.

## Word order — the verb goes last

<!-- topic: word-order -->

English speakers often keep English word order in Malayalam. Malayalam is subject – object – verb, so the verb comes last: ഞാൻ ചായ കുടിക്കുന്നു (njaan chaaya kudikkunnu, I tea drink = I drink tea), not "ഞാൻ കുടിക്കുന്നു ചായ". Time and place usually come after the subject and before the verb: ഞാൻ നാളെ കൊച്ചിയിൽ പോകും (njaan naale Kochiyil pokum, I will go to Kochi tomorrow). Words like "in", "to" and "from" are endings after the noun, not words before it: വീട്ടിൽ (veettil, in the house).

## Dropping "is" — ആണ് and ഉണ്ട്

<!-- topic: to-be -->

Learners either leave out the Malayalam "is" where it is needed or use the wrong one. ആണ് (aanu) links "X is Y" and can be dropped in short phrases: എന്റെ പേര് ആശ (ente peru Asha, my name is Asha). But for existence, location and "to have", you need ഉണ്ട് (undu): അമ്മ വീട്ടിലുണ്ട് (amma veettilundu, mother is at home); എനിക്ക് ഒരു ചേട്ടനുണ്ട് (enikku oru chettanundu, I have an elder brother). Leaving out ഉണ്ട് makes the sentence incomplete, and using ആണ് for "there is" is wrong.

## Long and short vowels

<!-- topic: pronunciation; skill: pronunciation; level: beginner -->

English speakers often shorten or lengthen Malayalam vowels by accident, and this can change the word. മല (mala, mountain) vs മാല (maala, necklace); കട (kada, shop) vs കാട (kaada, quail); ഇവിടെ (ivide, here) has short i, but ഈ (ee, this) is long. Hold long vowels about twice as long: വാ (vaa, come), നീ (nee, you), പൂ (poo, flower). Vachan writes long a, i and u doubled (aa, ee, oo), but writes long ഏ and ഓ simply as e and o (പേര്, peru), so check the script.

## Retroflex vs dental sounds

<!-- topic: pronunciation; skill: pronunciation -->

Malayalam distinguishes dental sounds (tongue at the teeth) from retroflex sounds (tongue curled back), and English speakers tend to use one in-between sound for both. Dental: ത (tha) in തല (thala, head), ന in നന്ദി (nandi), ല in പുലി (puli, leopard). Retroflex: ട in പട്ടി (patti, dog), ണ in പണം (panam, money), ള in പുളി (puli, sour). The ല / ള pair matters most for meaning. Remember too that റ്റ is said "tt" (കാറ്റ്, kaattu, wind) and ന്റ is "nt" (എന്റെ, ente, my).

## Literal translations that sound odd

<!-- topic: phrases; skill: conversation -->

Some English phrases translated word for word sound odd in Malayalam. "You're welcome" is not സ്വാഗതം (swaagatham); say അതിനെന്താ (athinenthaa). "Goodbye" is not a final farewell; say പോയിട്ട് വരാം (poyittu varaam, I'll go and come back). "Please" ദയവായി (dayavaayi) in every request sounds stiff; use -ഊ (-oo) or -ആമോ? (-aamo?). "I love you" as ഞാൻ നിന്നെ സ്നേഹിക്കുന്നു sounds like a film; എനിക്ക് നിന്നെ ഇഷ്ടമാണ് is more natural. "Good morning" സുപ്രഭാതം is formal; in speech people say "good morning". Thanking close family with നന്ദി can sound distant.

## Answering "yes" — അതെ, ഉവ്വ്, ഉണ്ട്

<!-- topic: questions -->

English speakers tend to answer every question with അതെ (athe, yes), but Malayalam usually repeats the key word. "Will you come?" വരുമോ? — വരും (varum, yes, I'll come). "Is there tea?" ചായ ഉണ്ടോ? — ഉണ്ട് (undu). "Did you eat?" കഴിച്ചോ? — ഉവ്വ് (uvvu) or കഴിച്ചു (kazhichchu). അതെ fits "is it so?" questions: ഇത് നിങ്ങളുടെ ബാഗാണോ? — അതെ (yes, it is). Casual speech often uses ആ (aa) or ശരി (shari).

## Using അവൻ / അവൾ for elders

<!-- topic: pronouns -->

A common mistake is calling an older or respected person അവൻ (avan, he) or അവൾ (aval, she). In Malayalam these are for people your age or younger. For elders, teachers and people you respect, use അവർ (avar, they — respectful he / she) or അദ്ദേഹം (addeham, he, formal). Examples: എന്റെ ടീച്ചർ വന്നു; അവർ നല്ല ടീച്ചറാണ് (ente teacher vannu; avar nalla teacheraanu, my teacher came; she is a good teacher). Possessives follow: അവരുടെ (avarude, his / her — respectful).
