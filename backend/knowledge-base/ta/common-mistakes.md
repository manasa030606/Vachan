# Common mistakes English speakers make in Tamil

## Using நீ (nee) with strangers — formal vs informal "you"

<!-- topic: politeness; skill: conversation; level: beginner -->

The most common beginner mistake in Tamil is using நீ (nee, casual "you") with people you should respect. நீ is only for close friends your age, younger siblings and children; with a stranger, elder, teacher or shopkeeper it sounds rude. Use நீங்கள் (neengal), spoken நீங்க (neenga), and the matching verb: நீங்க எப்படி இருக்கீங்க? (neenga eppadi irukkeenga?, how are you?) — not நீ எப்படி இருக்க? Likewise use உங்கள் / உங்க (ungal / unga, your), not உன் (un). When unsure, always choose நீங்க — nobody is offended by too much politeness.

## Forgetting the polite command ending

<!-- topic: imperative -->

English speakers often give Tamil commands with the bare verb root — குடு (kudu, give!), வா (vaa, come!), சொல் (sol, say!). These are casual and sound bossy with adults. Add the polite ending: written -உங்கள் (-ungal), spoken -ங்க (-nga): குடுங்க (kudunga, please give), வாங்க (vaanga, please come), சொல்லுங்க (sollunga, please say). Adding the English word "please" (or தயவுசெய்து, thayavuseydhu) to a bare root does not fix it — the verb ending is what carries politeness in Tamil.

## Using அவன் / அவள் for elders

<!-- topic: pronouns -->

Tamil has casual and respectful "he / she". அவன் (avan, he) and அவள் (aval, she) are for children, friends and younger people. For parents, teachers, elders and anyone you don't know well, use the respectful அவர் (avar), spoken அவரு (avaru) for a man and அவங்க (avanga) for a woman or a respected person. The verb changes too: அவர் வந்தார் (avar vandhaar, he/she came, respectful) vs அவன் வந்தான் (avan vandhaan, he came, casual). Saying அவன் about your teacher would be impolite.

## Gender agreement — where Tamil changes and where it doesn't

<!-- topic: gender; skill: grammar -->

In Tamil, verbs change for gender only with he / she: அவன் போனான் (avan ponaan, he went), அவள் போனாள் (aval ponaal, she went). "I" and "you" forms are the same for everyone: நான் போனேன் (naan ponen, I went) is said by men and women. But some nouns of identity do change, and learners often forget: மாணவன் / மாணவி (maanavan / maanavi, male / female student), நண்பன் / தோழி (nanban / thozhi, male / female friend), சேர்ந்தவன் / சேர்ந்தவள் (serndhavan / serndhaval, "belonging to", e.g. நான் சென்னையைச் சேர்ந்தவள், I'm from Chennai — a woman speaking).

## Putting the verb in the middle — word order

<!-- topic: word-order -->

English speakers often keep English word order in Tamil: "நான் சாப்பிடுகிறேன் சாதம்" (I eat rice). Tamil is verb-final: subject – object – verb. Say நான் சாதம் சாப்பிடுகிறேன் (naan saadham saappidugiren, literally "I rice eat"); spoken நான் சாதம் சாப்பிடுறேன் (saappidren). Place, time and "to" words also come before the verb: நான் நாளைக்கு சென்னைக்குப் போறேன் (naan naalaikku Chennaikkup poren, I'm going to Chennai tomorrow). Question words sit just before the verb too: நீங்க எங்க போறீங்க? (neenga enga poreenga?, where are you going?).

## Adding a word for "is"

<!-- topic: word-order; level: beginner -->

Learners look for a Tamil word for "is" in sentences like "my name is Asha". There isn't one: என் பெயர் ஆஷா (en peyar Asha) is complete. Also இது தண்ணீர் (idhu thanneer, this is water), என் அப்பா டாக்டர் (en appaa daaktar, my father is a doctor). Adding இருக்கிறது here is wrong. Use இரு (iru, to be) only for location and state: அவர் வீட்டுல இருக்கார் (avar veettula irukkaar, he/she is at home), இது நல்லா இருக்கு (idhu nallaa irukku, this is good). Tamil also has no word for "a / the": ஒரு (oru) means "one", used only when it matters.

## Saying "I hungry" — feelings and wants use எனக்கு

<!-- topic: dative -->

A frequent mistake is translating "I am hungry" as நான் பசி (naan pasi, "I hunger") or "I want" as நான் வேண்டும். Tamil puts the person in the "to me" form எனக்கு (enakku) for hunger, wanting, needing, liking, knowing and having: எனக்குப் பசிக்குது (enakkup pasikkudhu, I'm hungry), எனக்கு டீ வேணும் (enakku tee venum, I want tea), எனக்குப் பிடிக்கும் (enakkup pidikkum, I like it), எனக்குத் தெரியும் (enakkuth theriyum, I know). For "you" it is உங்களுக்கு (ungalukku): உங்களுக்கு என்ன வேணும்? (what do you want?).

## Long and short vowels

<!-- topic: vowel-length; skill: pronunciation -->

English speakers often shorten Tamil long vowels, but length changes meaning. பல் (pal, tooth) vs பால் (paal, milk); கல் (kal, stone) vs கால் (kaal, leg); வலி (vali, pain) vs வாலி (Vaali, a name). Hold aa, ee, oo about twice as long as a, i, u. Tamil also has short எ / ஒ and long ஏ / ஓ: கொடு (kodu, give) has a short o, போ (po, go) a long one. Vachan's romanization writes ஆ ஈ ஊ as aa, ee, oo, so read doubled letters long: நாள் (naal, day), வீடு (veedu, house), பூ (poo, flower).

## Retroflex vs dental sounds

<!-- topic: retroflex; skill: pronunciation -->

Tamil separates dental sounds (tongue at the teeth) from retroflex ones (tongue curled back). Mixing them can change words. ல (l, dental) vs ள (l, retroflex): பல்லி (palli, lizard) vs பள்ளி (palli, school) — romanization looks the same, so check the script. த (th, dental) vs ட (t / d, retroflex): தம்பி (thambi) starts with a soft dental t, while டீ (tee, tea) has a curled-back t. ந / ன (dental n) vs ண (retroflex n): தண்ணீர் (thanneer, water) has the curled ண. And ழ (zh) is its own sound, as in தமிழ் (Thamizh).

## "th" is not the English th

<!-- topic: romanization; skill: pronunciation; level: beginner -->

In Vachan's Tamil romanization, "th" and "dh" stand for the dental த — a t / d with the tongue touching the back of the upper teeth — not the English "th" of "think" or "this". தண்ணீர் (thanneer, water) starts like a soft "t", and அது (adhu, it) has a soft "d". Likewise "zh" (ழ) is not an English z: it is the curled Tamil sound in தமிழ் (Thamizh). And "ndr" (ன்ற) in நன்றி (nandri) is said as written: nan-dri.

## Literal translations that sound odd

<!-- level: intermediate; skill: culture -->

Some English phrases don't translate word for word into natural Tamil. "Good morning" as காலை வணக்கம் (kaalai vanakkam) is used on TV and in messages, but in person people just say வணக்கம் (vanakkam). Saying நன்றி (nandri) for every small thing to family can sound distant. நான் உன்னைக் காதலிக்கிறேன் (I love you) is only romantic — never for parents or friends. "I miss you" has no exact Tamil verb; people say உன்னை மிஸ் பண்றேன் (unnai mis panren). And "How are you?" to a friend is often replaced by சாப்பிட்டியா? (saappittiyaa?, have you eaten?).

## Mixing written and spoken Tamil in one sentence

<!-- topic: diglossia -->

Tamil has a written (formal) style and a spoken style, and learners often mix them oddly: ரொம்ப நன்றாக இருக்கிறது combines spoken ரொம்ப (romba) with written நன்றாக இருக்கிறது. Pick one style per sentence. Spoken: ரொம்ப நல்லா இருக்கு (romba nallaa irukku, it's very good). Written: மிகவும் நன்றாக இருக்கிறது (migavum nandraaga irukkiradhu). For conversation, the polite spoken style (-ங்க endings, இருக்கு, வேணும்) sounds most natural; the written style is for reading, writing and formal speeches, and is always understood.

## Using one word for "brother" or "sister"

<!-- topic: family; type: vocabulary -->

English "brother" and "sister" are not enough in Tamil: you must say older or younger. அண்ணன் (annan, elder brother; called அண்ணா, annaa), தம்பி (thambi, younger brother), அக்கா (akkaa, elder sister), தங்கை (thangai, younger sister; spoken தங்கச்சி, thangachchi). The formal சகோதரன் (sagodharan, brother) and சகோதரி (sagodhari, sister) exist but sound bookish in conversation. These words are also used to address strangers: அண்ணா for a slightly older man, அக்கா for a slightly older woman, தம்பி for a younger boy.

## ஒன்று vs ஒரு — "one" before a noun

<!-- topic: numbers -->

Beginners often say ஒன்று டீ for "one tea". In Tamil, ஒன்று (ondru, spoken ஒண்ணு, onnu) is the counting form, used alone: எத்தனை? — ஒன்று (how many? — one). Before a noun, "one / a" is ஒரு (oru): ஒரு டீ (oru tee, one tea), ஒரு டிக்கெட் (oru tikket, one ticket), ஒரு நிமிஷம் (oru nimisham, one minute). Other numbers stay the same before nouns: ரெண்டு இட்லி (rendu idli, two idlis), மூணு பேர் (moonu per, three people). The noun after a number usually stays singular.

## எத்தனை vs எவ்வளவு — how many vs how much

<!-- topic: questions -->

Learners often mix up Tamil "how many" and "how much". எத்தனை (eththanai) is for things you count one by one: எத்தனை டிக்கெட்? (eththanai tikket?, how many tickets?), எத்தனை பேர்? (eththanai per?, how many people?). எவ்வளவு (evvalavu) is for prices, amounts, distance and time: இது எவ்வளவு? (idhu evvalavu?, how much is this?), எவ்வளவு தூரம்? (evvalavu thooram?, how far?). One exception to remember: clock time uses எத்தனை — எத்தனை மணிக்கு? (eththanai manikku?, at what time?).

## நாம் vs நாங்கள் — two kinds of "we"

<!-- topic: pronouns -->

Tamil has two words for "we", and English speakers often use the wrong one. நாம் (naam) includes the person you are talking to ("you and I"): நாம் போகலாம் (naam pogalaam, let's go — together). நாங்கள் (naangal, spoken நாங்க, naanga) excludes the listener ("my group, not you"): நாங்க நாளைக்கு வருவோம் (naanga naalaikku varuvom, we will come tomorrow — we, without you). Their possessives differ too: நம்ம ஊர் (namma oor, our town — yours and mine) vs எங்க ஊர் (enga oor, our town — not yours).

## வாங்க — "buy" or "come"?

<!-- level: elementary -->

A classic beginner confusion in Tamil: வாங்க (vaanga) has two meanings. In written Tamil it is the infinitive of வாங்கு (vaangu, to buy): காய்கறி வாங்கப் போறேன் (kaaygari vaangap poren, I'm going to buy vegetables). In spoken Tamil it is also the polite "please come", from வா (vaa, come): உள்ள வாங்க (ulla vaanga, please come in). Context makes the meaning clear — a shopkeeper calling வாங்க வாங்க! (vaanga vaanga!) is welcoming you, not telling you to buy.

## Two words for "no" — இல்லை vs வேண்டாம்

<!-- topic: confused-words; level: beginner -->

Tamil has two everyday words that English speakers both translate as "no". இல்லை (illai, spoken இல்ல, illa) means "no / not / there isn't": சாப்பிட்டீங்களா? — இல்ல (have you eaten? — no); தண்ணி இல்ல (there's no water). வேண்டாம் (vendaam, spoken வேணாம், venaam) means "I don't want it / no thanks": when someone offers tea, say வேண்டாம், நன்றி (vendaam, nandri, no, thank you). Saying இல்லை to an offer sounds like "there isn't". A third word, அல்ல (alla), is the written "is not": இது என் வீடு அல்ல (this is not my house).

## புரியும் vs தெரியும் — understand vs know

<!-- topic: confused-words -->

Beginners mix up Tamil புரி (puri, to understand) and தெரி (theri, to know). Use புரி for understanding something said or explained: எனக்குப் புரியல (enakkup puriyala, I don't understand); புரிஞ்சுதா? (purinjudhaa?, did you understand?). Use தெரி for knowing facts, people, skills and languages: எனக்குத் தெரியாது (enakkuth theriyaadhu, I don't know), எனக்குத் தமிழ் தெரியும் (enakkuth Thamizh theriyum, I know Tamil). Both take எனக்கு (to me). So "I don't understand Tamil" is எனக்குத் தமிழ் புரியல — said about what you just heard.

Also asked as: puriyala vs theriyala, understand vs know in Tamil, difference between puri and theri

## பிடிக்கும் vs பிடிச்சிருக்கு — like in general vs like this one

<!-- topic: confused-words; level: elementary -->

Tamil has two forms of "like". பிடிக்கும் (pidikkum) is for general, lasting likes: எனக்குக் காபி பிடிக்கும் (enakkuk kaapi pidikkum, I like coffee). பிடிச்சிருக்கு (pidichchirukku), written பிடித்திருக்கிறது (pidiththirukkiradhu), is for something you have just seen or tried: இந்தச் சட்டை எனக்குப் பிடிச்சிருக்கு (indhach sattai enakkup pidichchirukku, I like this shirt). Negatives: பிடிக்காது (pidikkaadhu, I don't like it in general) and பிடிக்கல (pidikkala, I don't like this one).
